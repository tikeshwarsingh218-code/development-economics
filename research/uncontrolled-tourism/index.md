### Uncontrolled tourism leads to the loss of intellectual property.

**Article 2026**

India’s mountainous regions, such as Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, and the northeastern states, are unable to provide employment opportunities commensurate with the growing aspirations of their residents due to difficult geographical conditions. However, these snow-capped mountains are crucial for tourism. Tourism Department data shows that domestic tourism has increased significantly in recent decades (Ministry of Tourism, Government of India, 2022). With the exception of a few states, almost all mountainous states saw 70 to 188% more domestic tourist arrivals in 2021 compared to 2020 (Ministry of Tourism, Government of India, 2022; Kaushik & Thakur, 2024). Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh saw increases of approximately 350 and 4394%, respectively (Ministry of Tourism, Government of India, 2022). This growth is driven by political efforts and government provision of infrastructure such as roads. The rise in domestic tourism is also an indicator of the country’s growing economic prosperity, as only with economic stability can the population afford to spend on luxuries like tourism.

Although the mountains cannot attract large factories and industries, these same mountain ranges attract tourists and bring economic prosperity to the plains for their residents. However, mountainous regions pay a heavy price for promoting tourism (Capocchi et al., 2019). Scenic tourism relies on modern amenities such as roads, internet, ropeways, resorts, hotels, and waste management, and a breakdown in any of these facilities adversely impacts the tourist experience. Small towns like Manali and Kullu host approximately three to six times their permanent population each year, yet the massive volume of waste generated by these tourists is simply too large to handle in their waste management systems (Census of India, 2011; The Tribune, 2024a, 2024b). Consequently, heaps of garbage can often be seen along the banks of rivers and their tributaries (Nüsser et al., n.d.; Down To Earth, 2026). Thus, deforestation for roads and a lack of waste management destroy the very beauty that attracts tourists to the mountains.

The negative impact of tourism based solely on superficial natural beauty has also been seen on education and social intelligence. The 2021 National Achievement Survey conducted by the Department of School Education and Literacy revealed a decline in the quality of education in almost all mountainous regions (National Council of Educational Research and Training, 2021; Press Trust of India, 2025). While this decline may be due to many reasons, economists consider the decline in educational quality in states like Himachal Pradesh to be a symptom of "Dutch Disease" (Corden & Neary, 1982; Corden, 1984). Dutch disease is not a physical or mental illness, but rather refers to the disruption of development in another region caused by sudden economic growth in a specific region (Corden & Neary, 1982). Therefore, when tourism increases, it often draws resources, such as skilled labor, from other industries (Alkhathlan et al., 2019).

In India, over the past two decades, due to economic stability, improved infrastructure, and social media, tourism in mountainous regions has seen a significant boom, attracting and engaging rural youth. Indian mountainous regions attract tourists with their natural beauty. Unlike European nations, mountainous states have failed to attract domestic tourists with the cultural and historical heritage displayed in museums. States like Uttarakhand, Jammu and Kashmir, and Himachal Pradesh attract religious tourists due to the presence of some prominent temples, but their numbers are extremely low. Social media is also dominated by novel and exotic experiences. A very small segment of people visit the mountains to experience the unique traditions, culture, and lifestyle, and out of positive curiosity. Most trekking is conducted in the name of eco-tourism (Sati, 2018; Kuniyal et al., 2025). When quick money can be earned with a few hours of hard work, by becoming a tour guide, selling two-minute noodles at exorbitant prices, by drug trafficking, or by trekking, why would anyone pay for years of study? The lack of skilled labor due to the lack of higher education also impacts other industries. In many areas, tourism fuels inflation, forcing local citizens, who are the custodians of cultural heritage, to migrate (Mikulić et al., 2021; Mamgain & Reddy, 2018; Milano et al., 2019). The recent anti-tourism protests in Italy are indicative of this (Barry, 2024; Euronews with Associated Press, 2024; Wilson, 2025). There’s no doubt that mountain tourism is crucial and should be promoted. However, the growth of this industry needs to be managed very carefully. If mountain tourism relies not only on natural observation but also on the local culture and historical heritage, young people should study culture and history in universities. A tour guide should not only sell the crafts and handicrafts of their state but also understand the history behind them, their social significance, and their uniqueness, thus spreading knowledge. When a tourist wanders in an environment rich in knowledge, they will think twice before littering.

While the government is building roads, airports, and ropeways, it should also ensure that libraries and museums are built to preserve and teach folk art and knowledge, and make them so attractive that tourists are drawn there. As tourists learn about local culture, they will ask more questions of the tour guide and trekker, and the answers will be found in books and in the company of wise and learned people.
