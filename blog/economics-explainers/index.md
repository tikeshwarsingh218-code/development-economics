### Economics explainers

Short explanations of concepts that come up repeatedly in my coursework and research.

### What is Difference in Differences?

Difference in Differences is a method used to study changes in outcomes associated with an intervention, policy, or event. It compares the change over time for a group affected by the intervention with the change over time for a comparison group that was not affected. The method is useful when researchers want to estimate a causal effect using observational or quasi experimental data.

The central idea is to compare differences rather than simply comparing groups or periods. A major assumption is that, in the absence of the intervention, the treated and comparison groups would have followed similar trends over time. Researchers therefore need to examine the research setting, timing, treatment exposure, and underlying trends carefully.

### What is a Randomized Controlled Trial?

A Randomized Controlled Trial is an experimental research design in which individuals, households, firms, communities, or other units are randomly assigned to different groups. Usually, one group receives an intervention while another group serves as a comparison. Random assignment helps ensure that the groups are comparable before the intervention begins.

The difference in outcomes between the groups can then provide evidence about the effect of the intervention. In practice, however, an experiment involves much more than random assignment. Recruitment, treatment delivery, survey implementation, attrition, measurement, and compliance can all affect the quality of the evidence.

### What is causal inference?

Causal inference is the study of whether a particular intervention, exposure, or change actually causes a change in an outcome. This is different from simply identifying a correlation between two variables. For example, observing that education and earnings are positively related does not by itself establish that additional education causes higher earnings.

The central challenge is the counterfactual. Researchers want to know what would have happened to the same unit under a different treatment or exposure. Since both outcomes cannot normally be observed for the same unit at the same time, empirical methods attempt to construct credible comparisons that approximate the missing counterfactual.

### What is selection bias?

Selection bias occurs when the units included in an analysis differ systematically from the population or comparison group that the researcher wants to study. The problem can arise when participation, treatment assignment, survey response, or inclusion in a dataset is related to characteristics that also affect the outcome.

Selection bias can make a relationship appear stronger, weaker, or even opposite to the relationship that exists in the broader population. Researchers therefore need to understand how observations entered the sample and whether the process of selection is related to the variables being studied.

### What is omitted variable bias?

Omitted variable bias occurs when an important variable is left out of a statistical model and that variable is related both to the explanatory variable of interest and to the outcome. In such a situation, the estimated relationship can partly capture the effect of the omitted factor rather than the relationship that the researcher is trying to measure.

For example, suppose education is related to earnings and ability affects both education and earnings. If ability is not adequately accounted for, the estimated relationship between education and earnings may reflect both education and differences in ability. The problem is therefore closely connected to causal interpretation.

### What are fixed effects?

Fixed effects are commonly used in panel data analysis to account for characteristics of observational units that do not change over time. For example, when studying individuals, firms, districts, or schools over several periods, some characteristics of those units may remain relatively stable but still affect the outcome.

Including fixed effects allows the analysis to focus on variation within the relevant unit rather than relying entirely on differences between units. This can help account for unobserved time invariant characteristics, although fixed effects do not automatically solve every source of omitted variable bias or establish causality.

### What is survey weighting?

Survey weighting is a method used to account for differences in the probability that observations are selected into a survey. Some people or households may have a higher probability of selection than others, and weights can be used so that the resulting estimates better represent the population from which the sample was drawn.

Weights need to be interpreted together with the broader survey design. Stratification, clustering, nonresponse, and other features of the sampling process can also affect estimation. Applying a weight mechanically without understanding how it was constructed can therefore lead to incorrect conclusions.

### What is a sampling frame?

A sampling frame is the underlying list, map, register, or other structure used to identify the population from which a sample is selected. It provides the practical basis for deciding which units can enter a survey. Depending on the study, the sampling frame might consist of households, individuals, villages, firms, schools, or other units.

The quality of a sampling frame matters because gaps or errors in the frame can affect who has a chance of being selected. If important parts of the target population are missing, the resulting sample may not adequately represent the population that the researcher intends to study.

### What is internal migration?

Internal migration refers to movement of people from one location to another within the same country. In India, this can involve movement between villages, towns, cities, districts, or states. People may move temporarily, seasonally, or for longer periods depending on employment, education, household circumstances, social networks, and other factors.

Migration is therefore not a single phenomenon with one explanation. Different forms of migration can have different economic consequences for workers and households. Studying internal migration requires attention to the reasons for movement, the duration of migration, the destination, and the labour market conditions faced by migrants.

### Why does migration happen?

Migration can occur because people expect better economic opportunities in another location. Differences in wages, employment opportunities, education, public services, household conditions, and local economic conditions can all influence migration decisions. Social networks can also reduce the costs and risks associated with moving.

Migration decisions are often made within households rather than by individuals acting completely independently. People may move to diversify household income, support family members, respond to seasonal employment opportunities, or invest in education and future earnings. The reasons for migration can therefore vary substantially across workers and places.

### What is labour market informality?

Labour market informality refers to economic activity and employment arrangements that operate outside some formal institutional structures and protections. Informal workers may have limited access to written contracts, social security, employment protections, or other forms of formal labour regulation.

Informality is widespread and can take many forms. Workers may be employed by informal firms, work without formal contracts within otherwise formal organisations, or operate independently. Understanding informality therefore requires looking at both workers and firms and at the institutional environment in which employment takes place.

### What is human capital?

Human capital refers to the knowledge, skills, health, abilities, and other characteristics that can affect an individual's productivity and economic opportunities. Education and training are common examples, but human capital is broader than formal schooling alone. Health and accumulated work experience can also influence what people are able to do.

Human capital is important in development economics because differences in skills, health, and knowledge can contribute to differences in productivity and earnings. Investments in education, training, health, and early childhood development can therefore affect both individual outcomes and broader economic development.

### What is structural transformation?

Structural transformation refers to changes in the composition of an economy as development progresses. Historically, many economies have experienced a movement of workers and economic activity from agriculture toward manufacturing and services, accompanied by changes in productivity, urbanisation, and employment.

The process is not identical across countries. The timing and scale of transformation depend on technology, trade, institutions, infrastructure, human capital, and labour markets. Understanding structural transformation therefore requires examining how sectors change and how workers and firms respond to those changes.

### What is state capacity?

State capacity refers broadly to the ability of public institutions to implement policies, provide services, collect information, raise revenue, enforce rules, and carry out administrative functions. Capacity can vary across governments, departments, regions, and levels of administration.

State capacity is important because policies depend not only on their formal design but also on implementation. A programme may have clearly defined objectives but produce different outcomes if administrative systems have difficulty identifying beneficiaries, delivering services, maintaining records, or monitoring implementation.

### What is political economy?

Political economy studies how political institutions, incentives, power, and economic interests interact to shape policies and economic outcomes. It asks not only what a particular policy does but also why certain policies are adopted, how institutions influence decisions, and how different groups affect the allocation of resources.

Political economy can be useful for understanding outcomes that cannot be explained solely by market forces or technical policy design. Institutions, political incentives, collective action, information, and distributional conflicts can all influence economic decisions and the implementation of public policy.

### What are information frictions?

Information frictions arise when people do not have complete, accurate, or easily accessible information. In labour markets, workers may not know which firms are hiring or what wages different employers offer, while firms may have limited information about potential workers and their characteristics.

These information gaps can affect decisions even when suitable opportunities exist. Workers may remain in low productivity employment, firms may have difficulty finding suitable employees, and transactions may take longer or involve greater search costs. Information can therefore influence labour market outcomes independently of wages and skills.

### What is a public good?

A public good is a good or service with characteristics that make it difficult to exclude people from using it and where one person's use does not necessarily reduce the amount available to others. National defence and some forms of public information are commonly used as examples.

Public goods create a challenge for markets because individuals may have incentives to benefit from the good without contributing to its provision. This can result in under provision when decisions are left entirely to private markets. The characteristics of the good and the institutional setting determine the extent of this problem.

### What is an externality?

An externality occurs when an action by one person, household, or firm affects another person's welfare or production without that effect being fully reflected in the market transaction. Pollution is a common example because production or consumption can impose costs on people who are not directly involved in the transaction.

Externalities can lead to inefficient outcomes when private decisions do not account for the full social costs or benefits of an activity. Policy responses can include taxes, subsidies, regulation, property rights, information, or other institutional arrangements depending on the nature of the external effect.

### What is the Coase Theorem?

The Coase Theorem is an idea in economics concerning how parties affected by an externality may negotiate over the allocation of resources. Under highly restrictive conditions, including well defined property rights and very low transaction costs, bargaining can lead parties to an efficient outcome regardless of the initial allocation of rights.

The conditions are important because real world transactions often involve significant costs. Information problems, bargaining costs, enforcement difficulties, large numbers of affected parties, and unequal access to resources can all make private negotiation difficult. The theorem is therefore useful for understanding the role of transaction costs and institutions.

### What is a carbon market?

A carbon market is a system in which rights or obligations associated with greenhouse gas emissions can be traded. Depending on the design, participants may buy and sell emissions allowances or credits associated with reductions or removals of emissions.

The basic economic idea is to attach a price to emissions so that firms and other actors face incentives to reduce pollution. The effectiveness of a carbon market depends on its institutional design, including how emissions are measured, how permits or credits are allocated, how compliance is monitored, and how the market is regulated.

### What is climate adaptation?

Climate adaptation refers to actions that reduce the harmful effects of climate change or help people, communities, firms, and ecosystems adjust to changing environmental conditions. Examples include changes in agricultural practices, infrastructure investment, water management, disaster preparedness, and changes in where economic activities are located.

Adaptation decisions involve both economic and social considerations. Different households and communities have different resources and constraints, which means their ability to respond to climate risks can vary. Understanding adaptation therefore requires attention to income, institutions, information, infrastructure, technology, and local environmental conditions.

### What is spatial inequality?

Spatial inequality refers to differences in economic and social conditions across geographic areas. These differences can occur between states, districts, rural and urban areas, neighbourhoods, or other geographic units. Income, employment, education, health, infrastructure, and access to public services can all vary across space.

Spatial inequality is important because location can influence economic opportunities and access to resources. Geographic differences may reflect historical institutions, infrastructure, natural conditions, market access, public investment, or differences in local economic activity. Studying these patterns requires both economic analysis and careful attention to geography.
